SUPPORTING ARTICLE · RISK MANAGEMENT STRATEGY FOR IT DELIVERY

From uncertainty to controlled delivery

A practical companion to the 11-module course. Use this guide to connect the methods into one repeatable way to spot uncertainty, make proportionate decisions and keep delivery moving.

Risk management is a decision system

A useful risk process does more than collect threats in a register. It gives a team a shared way to describe uncertainty, compare exposure, decide who can act, fund a response and communicate when a decision is needed. The steps only work when they reinforce one another.

Start with a clear cause–event–effect statement. Assess the risk using agreed criteria and evidence. Compare residual exposure with tolerance. Select a response that addresses the cause, assign an owner and actions, then review triggers and report the decision required.

MODULE 1

Foundations of risk management

A risk is an uncertain future event that could affect an objective. An issue has already happened and needs to be managed as current work. Keeping that distinction clear helps teams respond before uncertainty becomes disruption.

Use it in practice: Use one shared lifecycle: identify, assess, plan, act and review. Record the cause, event and effect, then give each risk an owner, review date and next action.

MODULE 2

Risk categories

Categories make a register easier to analyse and help the right people contribute. The primary category should describe the root cause. Secondary categories can capture the ways that cause may affect delivery, such as cost, schedule, security or reputation.

Use it in practice: Categorise the cause first. Avoid choosing a category just because one consequence feels more serious.

MODULE 3

Objective scoring: probability, impact and proximity

The matrix combines probability and impact into a score. Proximity adds urgency: it tells the team how soon a risk could occur and how often to review it. It does not change the probability-times-impact score.

Use it in practice: Score using the agreed criteria and evidence. Use the highest relevant impact dimension so a severe effect is not hidden by a low financial estimate. Treat the attached matrix as illustrative and align its bands to your organisation.

MODULE 4

Risk tolerance and appetite

Appetite is a strategic position; tolerance is a measurable boundary for a specific type or level of exposure. Decisions should use residual risk after planned controls, rather than the initial score alone.

Use it in practice: Compare residual score with the relevant tolerance. Above tolerance calls for more mitigation or an authorised decision; within tolerance still needs an owner and monitoring.

MODULE 5

Cost of the risk and cost of mitigation

Expected monetary value (EMV) gives a probability-weighted estimate of exposure: probability multiplied by the cost if the risk occurs. Comparing the reduction in EMV with mitigation cost supports a transparent choice, while recognising that legal, safety, client and strategic factors may also matter.

Use it in practice: Write down the assumptions. A positive expected financial benefit supports mitigation, but it does not replace judgement about non-financial impact or risk appetite.

MODULE 6

Risk response strategy

The response should address the cause of the risk and fit the delivery objective. A response is not complete until it becomes a funded, owned plan with dated actions, evidence of progress and clear triggers for escalation.

Use it in practice: Choose deliberately among avoid, reduce, transfer, share and accept. For risks needing recovery, define measurable milestones in a Path to Green plan.

MODULE 7

Risk budget and contingency

Contingency is linked to identified risks and should be traceable to its purpose. Management reserve covers uncertainty that has not been identified and is governed separately. Clear draw-down and release rules prevent contingency becoming an untracked spending pot.

Use it in practice: Name the risks, record the estimate and approval route, and release unused contingency when its risk can no longer occur.

MODULE 8

Governance and ownership

Ownership answers who manages the risk. Escalation answers who must be informed or asked to decide, and when. A risk can be escalated while its operational owner remains the same. A Designated Authority Matrix (DAM) makes ownership consistent; an escalation plan defines the route and trigger.

Use it in practice: Give every risk one accountable owner. Record the escalation trigger, recipient, timeframe and decision needed.

MODULE 9

Commercial risk and Working at Risk (W@R)

Working at Risk means starting work before a signed contract or firm commitment to pay. It can protect a delivery schedule but creates commercial exposure. A controlled decision makes the exposure visible and limits how far work can proceed without contractual cover.

Use it in practice: Before any W@R start, quantify exposure, secure written approval, set a spend cap and stop-work date, and report against those limits.

MODULE 10

Communication and reporting

Good reporting helps its audience act. Executives need the exposure, movement in the risk picture and the decision or support required. Delivery teams need owners, actions, due dates and triggers. Tailor the level of detail without changing the underlying facts.

Use it in practice: Put the decision and exposure first. Keep the register as the source of truth and show changes that affect delivery choices.

MODULE 11

Putting it all together

The framework works as a connected system. In the Project Meridian example, a delayed API specification becomes useful to manage only when its cause and effect are clear, its score is evidenced, a proportionate response is funded and owned, and the team knows when to escalate.

Use it in practice: At each review, check whether the evidence, score, owner, actions, contingency, escalation route and report still agree.

One final review before you report

For each significant risk, ask: Is the uncertainty clearly stated? Is the score supported by evidence? Is the residual exposure within tolerance? Does the response address the cause? Is one person accountable? Are the trigger, decision authority and next review clear?

If those answers agree, the register, mitigation plan, contingency and report are telling the same story. That is when risk management becomes a practical control on delivery rather than a periodic paperwork exercise.
